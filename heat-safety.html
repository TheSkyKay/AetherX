Heat safety · India

Stay safe in extreme heat

Extreme heat can make anyone unwell. This page explains, in plain language, how to prepare, what to do on a hot day, and when to get help fast.

EmergencyHeatstroke is a medical emergency

If someone is confused, has a seizure, faints or is unconscious, or has very hot skin — call 112 now. Do not wait to see if they improve.

1Before the heat peaks

A few small jobs done in advance make the hottest days much easier to get through.

Prepare in advance

  • Check official local alerts. Look at the India Meteorological Department (IMD) warning for your district before you plan the day. A weather app's forecast is not an official warning.
  • Plan outdoor work for cooler hours. Do most outdoor work, errands and travel early in the morning or in the evening, when it is usually cooler.
  • Keep water available. Keep drinking water at home, at work and in your bag, and refill it during the day.
  • Identify a nearby cool place. Choose the coolest room at home, a shaded spot, or a local cool space. Ask locally which public places are open — this page has no verified list of cooling centres.
  • Check on people who may need help. Older adults living alone, people with a disability or a long-term illness, pregnant women, babies, and people who work outdoors.

Keep your home cooler

  • During the day, close windows, curtains and shutters when it is hotter outside than inside, especially in direct sun. Open them again at night when the outside air is cooler.
  • Turn off lights and electrical equipment you are not using — they add heat to a room.
  • Use a fan to move air, but above about 40 °C a fan can make the body hotter (WHO). At those temperatures, wet a cloth or your skin with cool water instead.
  • Wipe your face and body with a damp cloth, take a cool shower, or put your feet in cool water.
  • When cooking, open a window or use an exhaust fan to send steam and heat outside, and cook in the cooler part of the day when you can.
  • Keep the room where you sleep ventilated, and use light cotton bedding.

Make a simple plan

Decide who you will call, whose room is coolest, where medicines are kept, and what you will do if the power goes out. Make sure children and older adults know 112 and your family's phone numbers.

2During extreme heat

The aim is simple: keep the body cool and keep drinking. Change what you do, not just what you wear.

Everyday actions

  • Rest in shade or a cool place. Spending 2–3 hours of the day somewhere cool helps the body recover (WHO).
  • Drink water regularly through the day, before you feel thirsty, and keep water within reach.
  • Wear light, loose clothing in light colours. Cover your head, and carry an umbrella or cap.
  • Reduce strenuous activity during the hottest hours. Take frequent breaks in shade, and do not push through dizziness or weakness.
  • Avoid alcohol in the heat — it increases fluid loss.
  • Never leave children or pets in a parked vehicle, even for a few minutes with the windows down.
There is no single water amount that suits everyone. How much you need depends on your body, your work, the weather, your age and your medicines. That is why this page does not give one fixed amount.
Medical fluid restrictions: if a clinician has told you to limit fluids because of a heart, kidney or liver condition, follow their advice rather than general guidance.

👷 Outdoor workers

Start early or work in the evening, take a shade break every hour, and work in pairs so you can watch each other. Stop and cool down if you feel dizzy, weak or headachy. Take oral rehydration solution only as advised by a health worker.

🚌 Travelling

Carry water, avoid travelling in the hottest part of the day, and never leave children or pets alone in a vehicle. On buses and trains, check on older people and children around you.

🧒 Children

Encourage play in shade and away from midday sun, offer water often, and watch for unusual tiredness, irritability or drowsiness. Children can overheat faster than adults.

🧓 Older adults and people with disabilities

Check on them twice a day. Keep water within reach, help them get to a cooler place, store medicines somewhere cool, and remind anyone who cannot get water themselves to drink.

🐕 Pets and animals

Provide shade and clean water, walk them on cooler surfaces rather than hot pavement, never leave them in a vehicle, and skip long walks in the hottest hours.

💊 Medicines and health conditions

Some medicines affect sweating or fluid balance. If you take regular medicines or have a chronic condition, ask your clinician how to manage the heat, and keep medicines below about 25–30 °C where the label says so.

3Recognise heat illness

Early signs need cooling straight away. Confusion, a seizure or unconsciousness means call 112 immediately.

Early signs

Heat exhaustion

  • Heavy sweating, with skin that feels cool and clammy
  • Weakness and unusual tiredness
  • Dizziness or feeling light-headed
  • Headache
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Cramps in the arms, legs or stomach
  • Being very thirsty
  • Irritability

Skin colour changes are harder to see on brown or black skin, so go by the symptoms. If someone is not better after 30 minutes of cooling, get medical advice.

Emergency

Heatstroke — call 112 now

  • Confusion, slurred speech or strange behaviour
  • Seizure (fit) or body stiffness
  • Fainting or unconsciousness
  • Very hot skin — whether sweating or dry
  • Fast heartbeat or fast breathing
  • Body temperature of 40 °C or more
  • No improvement after 30 minutes of cooling

Not everyone sweats. Skin can be very hot and dry. Heatstroke can worsen quickly and needs emergency care.

4What to do

With heatstroke, every minute matters. Call for help first, then start cooling while you wait.

While help is on the way

  1. Call 112 or the nearest emergency department. Say where you are, how many people are affected, and what you can see.
  2. Move the person to the coolest shaded place you can reach.
  3. Loosen or remove unnecessary clothing such as a jacket, shoes or socks.
  4. Cool the skin with cool wet cloths, a sponge or spray of water, and fanning. Wrapped cold packs at the neck, armpits or groin help too.
  5. Give small sips of water only if the person is fully awake and can swallow safely.
  6. If they are unconscious but breathing, place them on their side in the recovery position. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR and tell 112.
  7. Stay with them and keep cooling until help arrives.

Do not

  • Give food or drink to someone who is unconscious, drowsy, or unable to swallow safely.
  • Give medicines, paracetamol, alcohol or home remedies instead of urgent medical care.
  • Put ice directly on the skin — wrap it in a cloth first.
  • Leave the person alone, or send them away on their own.
  • Make them walk, work or return to the heat before they have recovered.

If the signs are mild and the person is awake

Rest them in a cool place, loosen clothing, cool the skin and offer small sips of water or an oral rehydration drink. They should start to feel better within about 30 minutes. If they do not improve, get worse, or are older, pregnant, a young child or living with a long-term illness, seek medical advice straight away. Avoid heavy work in the heat the next day.

No medicine or home remedy replaces urgent medical care. This page does not recommend any medicine or treatment for heat illness.

Estimate and official warning are different

  • On this page: a forecast of how hot it may feel, calculated from the Open-Meteo weather model. Use it to plan your day, not as an alert.
  • IMD: the official heatwave or heat warning for a district. Local authorities, hospitals and the media act on these.

Emergency and local help

  • 112 — India's national emergency number for police, fire and ambulance. It is free and works 24 hours a day.
  • 108 / 102 — the National Ambulance Service numbers. They operate in most states and union territories, but availability and response vary by district. You can also ask 112 for an ambulance.
  • Cooling centres, shelters or local helplines: this page has no verified information for your area, so it does not list any. Ask your municipal office, district health office, or call 112.

6About this page

This page gives general safety information for the public. It is not a diagnosis, not personalised medical advice, and it does not replace a clinician, emergency services or instructions from local authorities. Following it cannot guarantee that someone stays well.
