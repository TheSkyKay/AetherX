How this works

Assumptions, limitations, and what would be needed before any of these numbers could be used for a real local decision.

Prototype · unvalidated

The score

Three components are each scored 0–100 and combined with fixed weights:

R = 0.5 × H + 0.3 × E + 0.2 × V
Low 0–24Moderate 25–49High 50–74Extreme 75–100

Weights and cut-offs are proposed values. They are a transparent starting point for discussion with local health and disaster officials, not a validated index.

Hazard (H) terms

  • Daytime heat: daily maximum apparent temperature (°C), 32 → 0 and 47 → 100. Weight 0.45.
  • Night heat: daily minimum apparent temperature (°C), 24 → 0 and 34 → 100. Weight 0.25.
  • Humidity heat stress: daily maximum wet-bulb temperature (°C), 24 → 0 and 32 → 100. Weight 0.15.
  • Persistence: number of days with feels-like maximum ≥ 40 °C in the 3-day window, 0 → 0 and 3 → 100. Weight 0.15.

If wet-bulb data is unavailable, that term is dropped and the remaining weights are renormalised; the table notes this.

Exposure (E) terms

  • Population density (people/km²), 500 → 0 and 30,000 → 100, log scale. Weight 0.40.
  • Outdoor or heat-exposed workforce (% of workers), 0 → 0 and 60 → 100. Weight 0.35.
  • Schools and hospitals per 100,000 people, 0 → 0 and 400 → 100. Weight 0.25.

Vulnerability (V) terms

  • Children under 5 plus adults 60+ (% of population), 10 → 0 and 30 → 100. Weight 0.25.
  • Adults with a chronic illness proxy (% of adults), 10 → 0 and 40 → 100. Weight 0.20.
  • Households in non-permanent or heat-prone housing (%), 0 → 0 and 50 → 100. Weight 0.20.
  • Households without piped water at home (%), 0 → 0 and 60 → 100. Weight 0.15.
  • Households without a fan or air conditioning (%), 0 → 0 and 60 → 100. Weight 0.20.

Weather measures — what is and is not shown

  • Air temperature (2 m): the standard forecast temperature in °C.
  • Apparent temperature: a feels-like measure combining temperature, humidity, wind and solar radiation. It is not WBGT.
  • Wet-bulb temperature: supplied by Open-Meteo, derived from temperature, humidity and pressure. It reflects how well sweat can cool the body, and is one heat-stress indicator — not a personal risk measure.
  • WBGT is not shown. A valid WBGT needs dry-bulb, natural wet-bulb and globe temperature (solar radiation and air movement). A 2 m air-temperature forecast does not provide those inputs, so any WBGT derived from temperature alone would be misleading.

Data sources

  • Forecast weather: Open-Meteo forecast API — air temperature, relative humidity, apparent temperature, wet-bulb temperature, UV index. Global gridded model output, not a local station. Dates and hours are local to the selected place.
  • Observed/past weather: Open-Meteo archive API (ERA5 reanalysis), roughly 9–25 km grid, available with a delay of several days.
  • Place search: Open-Meteo geocoding API, worldwide. Any city or town can be selected, and the results list shows region and country so same-named places stay distinguishable.
  • Exposure and vulnerability: bundled illustrative placeholders. The named sources below are what a real deployment would use; no values here are taken from them.

Assumptions

  • One weather grid cell represents the whole city or district; elevation, land use and the urban heat island are not resolved.
  • The score describes conditions on a single day plus a short persistence window. It has no lag, acclimatisation or multi-day mortality effect.
  • Exposure and vulnerability inputs are static across the day and apply to the whole area.
  • People can act on guidance: water, shade, rest, rescheduling and access to cooling.

Limitations

  • It does not predict illness, hospital admissions, deaths or economic loss.
  • It is not an official heatwave declaration and does not reproduce IMD criteria or alerts.
  • Gridded data smooths local extremes; a station or a top-floor room can be much hotter.
  • Indoor heat, housing quality and power cuts are only partly captured by the proxies.
  • No local calibration or validation has been performed on the weights, bands or thresholds.

What local validation would need

  • Ward-level station and automatic weather station data, including humidity and globe temperature.
  • IMD heatwave criteria and a historical archive of official alerts for the district.
  • Daily heat-illness counts from hospitals and 108 ambulance dispatch, plus mortality registry data by date and place.
  • Census and NFHS ward-level population, age structure, housing, water and cooling access.
  • Workforce and school calendars, cooling-centre locations, and electricity outage records.
  • Retrospective testing against past heat events (ROC/AUC, calibration curves), then tuning weights and thresholds with district health officers, followed by a prospective pilot.
